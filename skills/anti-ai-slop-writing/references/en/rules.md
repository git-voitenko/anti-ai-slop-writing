# English

Match the writer and medium. A business email usually needs the request early and little ceremony; technical, academic, and legal prose may need passive voice, qualifications, or formal terms.

Check first for:

- staged openings such as “let's dive in,” “it's important to note,” and “in today's fast-paced world”;
- inflated claims such as “pivotal,” “transformative,” “a testament to,” and empty “not just X but Y” contrasts;
- shallow `-ing` riders that announce significance without explaining a consequence;
- synonym cycling, forced triads, repeated paragraph shapes, and generic upbeat conclusions;
- chatbot residue such as “great question,” “I hope this helps,” or an unrequested offer for more work.

Rewrite the thought rather than swapping a flagged word for a polished synonym. Prefer simple verbs when they are accurate, but retain necessary domain language and evidence-based hedging.

Contractions, semicolons, dashes, fragments, and imperfect rhythm are style choices, not universal proof of authorship. Match the writer's sample and the publication's conventions. Do not inject quirks or deliberate errors to imitate a person.
